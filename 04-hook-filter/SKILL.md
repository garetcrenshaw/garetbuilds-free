# Hook Filter

Turn a supplied script or transcript into a clear, specific promise and a body that delivers it. Diagnose with quoted evidence, then fix the draft unless the user requests analysis only. Be candid without shaming. Editorial judgment cannot guarantee views, income, or virality; do not treat low views as proof that the hook failed.

## Establish the promise

- Identify the intended viewer, the concrete burden or desire, and the question the video will answer. State the promise in one plain sentence.
- Use the supplied audience. Otherwise default to business owners, operators, builders, and curious people. For business content, make freedom concrete: fewer repeated replies, less chasing, or work that no longer waits on the owner. Do not force a niche or topic into an unrelated script.
- Work from the supplied material. If none is supplied, ask for the script, transcript, or video; do not pretend to have reviewed it. If only a hook is supplied, improve it and mark body relevance, buried details, and payoff timing as unassessable until the body is available.
- Preserve the source text. Distinguish a proposed rerecord from an edit possible with existing recorded words. Respect approved wording and full-take locks; provide suggestions separately when a lock prevents cuts.

## Run all six filters

For each check, return **PASS**, **REVISE**, or **UNKNOWN**, a short source quote or location, and the concrete reason. A pass needs evidence; do not invent a numerical quality score.

### 1. Specific, clear, direct

Can a stranger understand what this is about and why it matters on the first listen? Replace vague subjects such as “this,” broad benefits such as “change your life,” throat-clearing, and abstract claims with a concrete person, task, problem, or result. Prefer active verbs and one main thought. The opening must make sense without the title or previous videos.

### 2. Third-grade comprehension

Audit the entire spoken script, not only the hook. Use familiar words, short spoken thoughts, clear pronoun references, and an easy-to-follow chain of cause and effect. Replace or immediately explain jargon with an everyday example. Keep necessary names and accurate technical terms; simplify the explanation without changing the claim or sounding childish.

Paraphrase the idea internally in one plain sentence. If that requires missing context, repair the script. Read it as speech. Do not claim a certified reading grade from intuition or a readability score; a formula is only a signal, especially for short scripts and proper names.

### 3. Viewer-specific detail and stakes

Check for a relevant specific name, number, recognizable situation, or emotional stake. These are alternative ways to create specificity, not mandatory ingredients to cram together. Favor emotions grounded in the viewer's day: another evening answering the same question, work piling up, or the relief of getting time back.

Use only supplied or verified facts, results, and first-person experiences. Do not invent numbers, income, hours saved, brand mentions, fear, or family shame to make a hook punchier. If a missing fact is essential, flag the precise gap outside the recordable copy; otherwise use a supported concrete detail. Names help only when the viewer understands their relevance.

### 4. Strongest detail in the first ten seconds

Scan the full script for its most compelling true line, result, contradiction, scene, or detail. Quote it and identify its original location. If it appears after 10 seconds, bring it or a faithful, self-contained version into the opening and repair the transition into the body. If the existing hook is stronger, explain why and keep it.

Use actual timestamps when available. With plain text, estimate from the speaker's known pace; otherwise use 150 words per minute, roughly 25 words in ten seconds. Label all estimates, account for pauses or visual setup when known, and keep the candidate comfortably inside that window. Never label estimated timing as observed. Separate spoken timing from on-screen headline timing.

### 5. Every sentence serves the promise

Check every sentence against the opening's value. Keep it only if it establishes needed context, advances the question, provides proof, explains a causal step, delivers value, or lands the answer. Remove unrelated side stories, greetings, repeated setup, duplicate conclusions, and off-topic CTAs. Rewrite necessary bridges instead of cutting the explanation that makes the video understandable.

Show meaningful cuts with their reasons. Do not add a new lesson, offer, anecdote, or CTA to fill the space. If the opening promises something the body cannot support, narrow the opening rather than inflate the body.

### 6. Earn the payoff

Identify the hook's question, where the script answers it, and what reason remains to keep watching. An early result can be an effective hook if the method, proof, obstacle, or implication still has value. Keep useful early answers when they help comprehension.

If the opening exhausts the video's value and the body only repeats it, move a supported unanswered question forward, tighten repetition, and build toward its answer. Give useful value along the way. Do not conceal essential context, add “wait until the end,” or delay a simple answer merely to stretch runtime. When there is no deeper supported material, make a shorter video. The ending must pay off the exact promise made at the start.


## Three-layer opening (adapted from vyralcontent/content-skills `viral-hooks`, MIT)

Write the first 1-2 seconds as one unit of three layers, each able to stand alone:

- **First frame:** shows the result, the tension, or a before/after. It must make sense muted and paused. No greeting, logo, recap, or slow zoom. A recap of a previous episode is never the opening.
- **Spoken line:** 5-10 words, one claim, said once. Lead with what the viewer gets or risks.
- **On-screen text:** 3-7 words, using different words from the spoken line, carrying the promise for mute viewers.

Rules:
- Listeners are selfish. Every hook names what a stranger gets or avoids, in words a non-follower understands. No private tool names, agent names, or insider phrasing in the hook.
- Fix a vague tease by naming the category of the answer without giving it away ("the one file that..." not "this changed everything").
- The hook opens a question the body answers. A bait-and-switch is a hard fail.
- If a hook can't be written, the idea is probably weak. Go back to the premise before polishing.
- For trial reels: write 6-10 hooks across at least 3 types (result, obstacle, question, belief, confession), pick 4-5 to film back-to-back on the same body, and let the test decide.
- Note how the last line loops back to the first, which encourages rewatches.
- Ban: "Here's what nobody tells you", "The result?" reveals, "real talk" sincerity openers, punch lines added just for rhythm, invented stats, and promises of virality. Don't chop sentences to fake rhythm (at most 2 standalone fragments).
- If the user keeps a list of past posts, check it so the hook and body don't repeat one.

## Rewrite and verify

Choose one strongest opening and produce one tightened script by default. Offer alternatives only when they represent useful, distinct directions or the user asks. Keep the speaker's own voice; preserve their strongest authentic phrasing. Avoid generic guru copy, decorative profanity, manufactured urgency, and motivational slogans.

For new planned teaching scripts, use short recording blocks with one thought per block. Keep editorial notes and stage directions outside the spoken copy.

Rerun the six checks on the rewrite before delivery. Check especially that moving a line did not orphan a pronoun, duplicate the payoff, change a fact, or overpromise. Report any unresolved evidence gap rather than falsely passing it.

## Deliver

Keep the response useful and proportionate:

1. **Verdict and promise:** the biggest issue and what the viewer gains.
2. **Six-check audit:** a compact table with status, source evidence, and fix. Clearly distinguish the original diagnosis from any remaining issue in the rewrite.
3. **Recommended hook and tightened script:** make the complete script independently recordable; include the chosen hook once at its start. Label timing estimates.
4. **Meaningful cuts and remaining gaps:** concise notes; omit this section when empty.

If analytics are provided, separate observed retention or view counts from hypotheses. Use repeated comparable results to refine future hooks, without claiming that one rewrite or one post proves causation.
